About Hollow Blocks

A free falling-block puzzle game set on a Filipino construction site · Updated October 7, 2026

What it is

Hollow Blocks is the classic falling-block puzzle, rebuilt in 3D and set on a small construction site in a Filipino barangay at golden hour. The pieces are real building materials: bamboo (kawayan), concrete hollow blocks, red bricks (ladrilyo), corrugated iron (yero), plywood, floor tiles (baldosa) and adobe stone. Every row you clear raises the house next door, floor by floor, while the foreman, Kapatas, watches from beside the well and tells you how you're doing.

It plays in any modern browser on a computer or a phone, with a keyboard, touch controls or a game controller. There's nothing to install and no account is needed. Progress is saved in your browser; an optional account (just a name and a password) keeps it safe across devices and lets you join the weekly rankings.

Why a construction site?

In the Philippines, the grey concrete hollow block is everywhere. Most houses in a barangay are built from them, one row at a time, often by the family and their neighbours over many years, adding a floor whenever there's money for it. Watching a house go up block by block is a familiar sight, and it felt like a natural home for a game about stacking pieces into neat rows.

The game borrows other everyday things from Filipino life too:

  • Bayanihan is the old custom of neighbours lifting a whole bahay kubo (a bamboo house) on their shoulders to move it. In the game, clearing four rows at once is a "Bayanihan", and the neighbours carry a house across the screen to celebrate.
  • Kapatas means foreman. He hands out job orders in the Projects mode, cheers your good moves and grumbles at your mistakes, in Filipino and English.
  • Bagyo (typhoon) and lindol (earthquake) are real hazards on a Philippine building site, so they show up as game modes and contract twists: rain and floodwater push mud up from below, and an earthquake slides the whole stack sideways.
  • Kita (earnings) is your score, counted in pesos, and barya (coins) buy new looks in the shop.

What's in the game

  • Bahay (House): the main endless mode. Every ten rows is a new floor and a little faster; the sun sets as the house rises, and every fifth floor finishes a house for your collection book.
  • Klasiko (Classic): the pure puzzle at one speed, with no tools or effects, and its own ranking board.
  • Deadline: clear forty rows as fast as you can.
  • Bagyo (Storm): survive rising mud during a typhoon.
  • Karera (Race): two minutes, the most earnings wins.
  • Daily: the same pieces for everyone each day, with a result you can share.
  • Lingguhan (Weekly): a new twist every week, such as no hold piece, instant drops, or an earthquake every half minute.
  • Proyekto (Projects): thirty contracts across six barangays, each a job order with a goal and a twist, ending with a visit from the City Inspector.
  • Laban (Versus): race a computer rival side by side, sending mud rows to each other, and climb the six-rival Barangay League.
  • Tapatan (Head to Head): two players on one keyboard or two controllers.
  • Pagsasanay (Training): short lessons on advanced moves like T-spins and combos.

There's also a shop for block skins, outfits for Kapatas and new building sites; replays of your best runs with a ghost to race; a daily streak; and settings for every control, including remapping each key and controller button.

Languages

Hollow Blocks is written in Filipino, with a full English translation. Switch between them any time under Settings → Wika · Language.

Who made it

Hollow Blocks is made by Lemmuel Turaya, a developer in the Philippines, as part of a series of free browser games inspired by Filipino places and pastimes: street games like tumbang preso, a jeepney ride at night, a sungka match against Lola, and more, all collected at the Tambayan arcade. The 3D graphics use three.js; the foreman's motion capture comes from Mixamo; the sounds are from Kenney's free libraries; and the music is original.

The game is free to play. It may show an optional ad that you choose to watch in exchange for in-game coins; it never shows ads in the middle of a game. Read the privacy page for what's stored and why, or get in touch through the contact page.
